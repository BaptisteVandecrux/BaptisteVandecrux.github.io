2016 was my first ever expedition on the Greenland ice sheet—an intense and unforgettable experience. As part of the FirnCover project, I joined a diverse team of 11 scientists from four countries, led by Mike MacFerrin. We were dropped off at DYE-2 with everything we needed for a fully self-supported campaign: skidoos, fuel, camping gear, and all our scientific equipment. From there, we spent about ten days traversing across southern Greenland by snowmobile, covering hundreds of kilometers and stopping at key sites like KAN-U and EKT to drill firn cores, dig snow pits, service GPS compaction stations, and maintain weather stations.

The days were long and the work physically demanding, but the spirit of the team and the shared excitement for polar science carried us through. After completing the southern traverse, we were stormed in at DYE-2 before continuing the campaign with a ski-equipped Twin Otter aircraft. We flew to remote research sites including Crawford Point, Summit, and EastGRIP. At Summit, our team drilled what was likely the fastest 16-meter firn core in history. The expedition was a crash course in polar logistics, teamwork, and scientific field methods—and it sparked my lasting commitment to ice sheet research.
